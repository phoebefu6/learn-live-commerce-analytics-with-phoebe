Design the say

A two-hour LIVE is 120 minutes and every one of them has a job: pull people in, build the product, show it, anchor the price, ask for the order, give a reason to order now, answer the room. Across Lumen Glow's 60 streams each of those jobs has a measured value in orders per thousand viewer-minutes, and the values are not close: a CTA minute is worth more than six hook minutes. Tonight you learn what each minute is worth, why the cheap minutes still belong in the script, where urgency stops converting and starts being a policy risk, and you leave with a script card for a 12-minute selling cycle that a host can run and a finance director can read.

LiveA script is a sequence of minutes, each with a job3 min▶

Hosts talk about a stream as a performance: energy, flow, chemistry with the room. All real, none of it measurable. A producer talks about the same stream as a run sheet: at minute 4 we pin the serum, at minute 9 we drop the price, at minute 11 we ask. That version is measurable, because every minute of every Lumen Glow stream has been labelled with what the host was doing in it, and every minute has a room size and an order count. Divide orders by the room and you have the value of that kind of minute, in orders per thousand viewer-minutes, comparable across a Wednesday with 1,579 people and a Monday with 498.

Once a script is a sequence of minutes with values, three things follow. You can see which minutes pay and which are the price of admission. You can see how long a cue keeps paying before it turns, which is the urgency finding. And you can hand a host a card instead of a mood: this minute's job is this, and here is what it is worth when it is done.

Real world

The Lumen Glow run sheet before this session had four rows: intro, product, offer, close. Nobody could say how long "offer" should be, so it ran until the room got restless, which on a good night was eight minutes of "almost gone". The run sheet after it has a minute column and a value column, and "offer" is two blocks: price, then urgency, two minutes, with a real countdown from the LIVE Flash Sale tool behind it.

Self-studyThe vocabulary tonight leans on4 min▶

The nine segment labels. Every minute of a stream is one of these, and the value table in Part 1 is built on them.

  • Hook - the opening minutes that earn the tap and the stay. Where a giveaway lives, when there is one.
  • Product build - the story of the product before it is shown: who it is for, what problem it answers.
  • Demo - the product in use, on camera. What session 4 measures from the picture side.
  • Price - the anchor: the 29 dollar serum, the 49 dollar two-pack, what the bundle saves.
  • CTA - the ask. "Tap the yellow basket" and its many cousins.
  • Urgency - the reason to order now rather than later: a flash sale countdown, a coupon that ends, a genuinely limited quantity.
  • Q&A - answering the room's comments by name.
  • Filler - talk that does none of the above. Chat about the weather, the host's day.
  • Dead air - nobody is talking. Usually a technical gap or a lost train of thought.

FABES is TikTok Shop Academy's own order for a product pitch: Features, Advantages, Benefits, Evidence, Scarcity. Read it as a script skeleton: build (features, advantages), demo and price (benefits, evidence), urgency (scarcity). Scarcity comes last and once.

The CTOR-vs-CTR split. What the host says mostly moves CTOR, click-to-order: the ask, the price, the reason to buy now all act on people who have already tapped the product card. What the host shows mostly moves CTR, the tap itself: the product in hand, the close-up, the price on screen. Tonight is the say and CTOR. Session 4 is the show and CTR.

Part 1 · covers the value of each segment, per viewer-minute and within stream

What each minute is worth

9 min live

Nine kinds of minute, one ladder. The unit is orders per thousand viewer-minutes, which puts the room in the denominator so a busy night and a quiet one sit on the same scale. Beside each value is how much of the average night that kind of minute takes up, because the script is not only what to say but how long to say it.

LiveThe within-stream effects, and what they mean for a script4 min▶

The ladder above is the plain average. Session 2 taught the sharper comparison: minutes against the other minutes of their own stream, so the room, the host and the hour cancel. Measured that way, in points of CTOR against the stream's own ordinary minute, the say looks like this.

Minute typeWithin-stream CTOR effectWhat it means for the script
CTA+6.50 pointsThe ask is the single most valuable sentence. It has to be said, on purpose, more than once a cycle
Short urgency (minutes 1-2 of a run)+4.93 pointsA genuine reason to buy now is nearly as strong as the ask itself, for two minutes
Price+3.78 pointsAnchor before you ask. The 49 dollar two-pack against the 29 dollar single does work here
Q&A+0.96 pointsSmall and positive. Answering the room by name pays, mostly through watch time
Filler-2.64 pointsChat about nothing costs conversion while it runs. Cut it or turn it into Q&A
Hook-3.18 pointsThe opening converts badly. It is not there to convert. See the next card
Dead air-4.72 pointsThe most expensive minute on the sheet. A producer's job is to make sure it never happens

Read the table as a budget. Every minute of the night is spent on one of these, and the sum of the effects times the minutes is roughly how the stream converts against its own potential. A script that adds two CTA minutes and removes two filler minutes has moved about 18 points of minute-CTOR from the wrong side of the ledger to the right one without changing anything about the host, the room or the product.

LiveWhy the hook is worth so little and still necessary3 min▶

The hook pays 1.98 orders per thousand viewer-minutes and converts 3.18 points below the stream's own average. If the script were only a list of values you would cut it. You would be wrong, because the hook's job is not to sell. The hook's job is entry and stay: it is the minutes when the feed is deciding whether to push the room and the viewer is deciding whether to give the stream nine minutes of their evening. Nobody buys a serum in minute two from a host they met in minute one. What they do in minute two is stay or leave, and everything after depends on which.

This is also where the giveaway lives, when there is one. Session 2 priced it: a giveaway minute converts 2.31 points worse and buys a bigger room. That is a hook doing a hook's job with a stronger tool, at a conversion cost the rest of the night pays. So the hook is in the script at its full weight, and the rule is about length, not presence: the hook's value is measured in who stayed, not in who bought, and the moment the room has arrived the script should move to build.

Real world

Priya's Monday 18:00, stream 16, the stream from session 1 that lost the GMV comparison and won the GPM one. No giveaway opener, a room that never passed 498, and a CTOR of 14.01 percent against a 60-stream average of 12.1. Her viewers stayed 9.82 minutes, above the 9.0 average, and her GPM was 2,309. A small room she did not buy, held longer than average and asked to buy at a higher rate than average. That is what a script does when the hook is short and the CTA is said on purpose.

Self-studyHow to ask: the affective CTA finding3 min▶

The Lumen Glow data prices the CTA as a minute. It does not say how the ask should be worded, because the label is the same whether the host says "tap the basket" or "you deserve to wake up to this skin, tap the basket". One published study does address it. Guo, Zhang, Goh and Peng (2025), in Production and Operations Management, report that affective calls to action, ones that speak to how the viewer will feel, raise orders in live streaming commerce, while cognitive calls to action, ones that list reasons and features, do not.

Read that with one flag attached: this is taken from the paper's abstract, not from the full text, so treat the direction as a lead worth testing in your own streams rather than a settled value. The practical script move it suggests is cheap to try: keep the features and evidence in the build and demo blocks where FABES puts them, and write the CTA itself in benefit-and-feeling language. Session 6 is where you would prove it worked on your own hosts.

Part 2 · covers the urgency cliff, and the wording the platform and the law read as deception

Urgency that converts, and urgency that stops converting

8 min live

Urgency takes 16.4 percent of the average Lumen Glow night, more than the CTA and the price together. It is the block hosts stretch when the room goes quiet, because it feels like selling. Measured minute by consecutive minute, it converts brilliantly for two minutes and then falls off a cliff, and the cliff is where the compliance risk begins.

LiveThe two-minute rule, and why a real cue is a real cue4 min▶

An urgency run is a stretch of consecutive minutes in which the host is giving the room a reason to order now. In its first minute the click-to-order rate is 15.12 percent; in its second, 15.44. Both are well above the 12.1 percent a Lumen Glow stream averages. In the third minute the rate is 8.30, and it stays around there, 8.33, 8.43, 9.09, for as long as the host keeps going. Within stream, the same pattern: the first two minutes of a run lift CTOR by +4.93 points against the stream's own ordinary minute, and every minute from the third onward lowers it by 1.84 points. The cue does not fade. It flips.

The reason is in what a scarcity cue is. A flash sale with a genuine countdown on screen, a coupon that genuinely expires at the top of the hour, a bundle with a genuinely limited number of units: these are facts about the offer, and a viewer who hears one either acts on it or decides not to. Two minutes is about how long that decision takes. A host who is still saying "almost gone" in minute five is no longer reporting a fact; the room can see that nothing has gone, and it re-reads the whole pitch as theatre, including the parts that were true. That is the 1.84 points, and it is also why the stretched version costs trust that the next flash sale has to buy back.

  • Real cue: LIVE Flash Sale with its countdown visible, LIVE Coupon with a stated end, Creator Exclusive Price for this stream only. All three are tools inside LIVE Manager; the platform shows the clock so the host does not have to keep claiming it.
  • Two minutes: state the cue, state what it is worth in dollars, ask, and move on to the next block. If the offer is still live at the next cycle, one sentence reminds the room; it does not need a run.
  • Stretched cue: anything past the second consecutive minute. Converts at about 8 percent, costs 1.84 points within stream, and from minute four it is the thing the next card describes.

Give the urgency block a clock, not a host. When the countdown is on screen from the Flash Sale tool, the host can say it once and let the platform's own timer carry it. A host carrying urgency by voice alone is the host most likely to stretch it, because the only way to keep it alive is to keep saying it.

LiveWhat a legal urgency claim is4 min▶

From minute four the stretched run is more than a conversion problem, because the sentences a host reaches for to keep it alive are the sentences the platform and the regulators have written down. The test in every one of these texts is the same: is the claim true. A genuine countdown is fine. A limited-time claim that is not limited is the named offence.

SourceWhat it namesScript consequence
TikTok Shop, How to Avoid Misleading ContentFlags "While stocks last", "This sale is for selected customers only", "Don't miss these big discounts", "We are having our largest sale!"None of these can be the urgency line. They claim scarcity or scale without a checkable fact behind them
TikTok Shop, Misleading Discount Content Guide (eff. 2026-08-31)"sale ends today" and "only 2 hours left" must be genuineA countdown line is allowed exactly when the countdown is real. Tie it to the Flash Sale clock
TikTok Shop, Content Policy (eff. 2026-09-17)Bans "false, misleading, unavailable, or expired prices or discounts"The price said on air must be the price on the product page, now
TikTok Shop UK, Content Policy (eff. 2026-01-09)Prohibits claiming the seller "will soon stop selling the product(s), when that is not the case""Last batch ever" is a claim about the future of the product. Do not make it unless it is true
UK DMCC Act 2024, Sch. 20 para 7 (in force 6 April 2025)"Falsely stating that a product will only be available for a limited time ... in order to elicit an immediate decision"A stretched urgency run to a UK audience is a described unfair practice, not a grey area
EU UCPD Annex I, item 7The same practice, with "very limited time"Same rule for EU viewers, in force for years

The pattern across seven texts from three jurisdictions and one platform is not subtle. A scarcity claim tied to a real, visible, checkable fact is a selling tool. The same words with nothing behind them are a listed deceptive practice. Minute three of an urgency run is where the fact usually runs out and the words keep going.

Self-studyThe banned absolutes, and how a claims checker catches them3 min▶

Urgency is one family of risky sentence. The other is the product claim, and TikTok Shop's misleading-content guide lists six absolutes it does not allow for a product like a serum: "100% effective", "Guaranteed results", "Works for everyone", "Permanent effect", "Instant results", "No side effects". They tend to appear in the same minutes as stretched urgency, because a host who has run out of true things to say about the offer reaches for stronger things to say about the product.

The builder track, session b4, turns this list into a checker: the stream's audio is transcribed with word-level timestamps, and every minute is scanned for the flagged urgency phrases and the six absolutes, plus the platform's flagged discount wording. A hit becomes a policy-risk flag on that minute, which is exactly the count session 2 quoted for Ren, 6.82 flags per stream. For a leader the useful part is not the code but the shape of the output: a list of minutes, with the sentence and the rule it tripped, ready to hand to the host with the script card. It is much easier to coach "you said 'guaranteed results' at minute 47" than "watch your claims".

Build-along

The script card

★ 20 min · everyone builds

A two-hour LIVE is roughly ten selling cycles of about 12 minutes each. Write one. One row per minute, three columns: the job, the measured value of that kind of minute, and the line the host says. Twenty minutes; the card is the artifact you take to the next run sheet review.

Lay out the twelve minutes in FABES order. Hook first but short, then build (features and advantages), demo and price (benefits and evidence), CTA, urgency (scarcity) for two minutes maximum, CTA again, Q&A. Put the value from Part 1 beside each block. Where a block has a within-stream effect too, write it in the same cell.

★ The 12-minute script card, one row per minute Min | Job | Value (orders / 1,000 viewer-min; within-stream CTOR) | What the host says 1 | Hook | 1.98; -3.18 pts. Job is stay, not buy | Who this is for, what we do tonight 2 | Product build | 4.51 | The problem the serum answers 3 | Product build | 4.51 | Features, advantages 4 | Demo | 7.72 | On the hand, on camera 5 | Demo | 7.72 | Benefits, in the viewer's words 6 | Price | 7.69; +3.78 pts | 29 single, 49 two-pack, the saving 7 | CTA | 11.57; +6.50 pts | Affective ask, basket named 8 | Urgency (1/2) | 5.83 avg; run 1-2 CTOR 15.12 / 15.44; +4.93 pts | Flash Sale clock on screen, stated once 9 | Urgency (2/2) | as above. HARD STOP at 2 minutes | Restate the saving and the clock, ask 10 | CTA | 11.57; +6.50 pts | Second ask, benefit-led 11 | Q&A | 3.68; +0.96 pts | Three questions by name 12 | Q&A / bridge | 3.68 | Answer, then bridge to next cycle Never: filler (1.40; -2.64 pts). Dead air (0.71; -4.72 pts) is a producer failure.

Place the urgency block at two minutes, maximum, and attach it to a real tool. Write down which LIVE Manager tool carries it: LIVE Flash Sale with its countdown, LIVE Coupon with its end time, or Creator Exclusive Price. If none of them is running that night, the urgency block is replaced by a second demo minute. Urgency with nothing behind it is not a block on this card.

Write the CTA in affective language. The features and evidence have already been said in minutes 3 to 5. The ask speaks to how the viewer will feel with the product, names the basket, and stops. Write two versions and pick the shorter one.

✗ The six-minute pitch that costs conversion and earns a flag Minutes 8-13: "Last chance, this is almost gone, we are having our largest sale ever, while stocks last, don't miss these big discounts. This serum is 100% effective, guaranteed results, works for everyone. Almost gone. Last chance. Only a few left. Almost gone..."
★ The two-minute genuine cue with a benefits-led ask Minute 8: "The Flash Sale clock is on your screen: the two-pack is 49 instead of 58 until it hits zero. That is two months of the serum for the price of one and a half." Minute 9: "Imagine waking up next month to the skin you had in your twenties. Tap the yellow basket while the clock is running." Then move to the second CTA and the room's questions.

Check every line against the flagged phrases. Read the card aloud and strike anything from the misleading-content list, anything that claims a limit that is not real, and any of the six absolutes. If a line survives only because you are sure it is true tonight, write the fact it depends on in the margin so the host knows what to check before saying it.

Add up the card. Count the minutes above the mean of 5.80 and the minutes below it. A cycle with seven above-mean minutes and no filler is a well-designed cycle. Then hand it to one host and ask them what feels wrong. The card is a measured starting point, not a script they read aloud; their job is to make it sound like them.

FABES is the order, not just the checklist. Features, Advantages, Benefits, Evidence, Scarcity. Scarcity is last for a reason: an urgency cue lands on a viewer who already knows what the product is, what it does for them, and what it costs. Said in minute two, before any of that, it is noise. Said in minute eight, once, with a clock behind it, it is 15 percent.

Homework

Before session 4

25 min
  1. Watch one 12-minute stretch of a recent stream from one of your hosts and label every minute with one of the nine segment labels. Write the labels in a column next to the script card you built tonight and mark where they differ.
  2. Time every urgency run in that stretch. Write down how many consecutive minutes each one lasted, and what the host said in the minute after the second one. If any run passed two minutes, write down whether there was a real clock behind it.
  3. Rewrite your host's current CTA in affective language, two versions, and read both to someone who has never seen the product. Keep the one they can repeat back.
  4. Open the LIVE Flash Sale tool in LIVE Manager and set up a test countdown without going live. Note what the viewer sees on screen. That is the clock your urgency block will point at from now on.
Check yourself

Three questions

Per thousand viewer-minutes, which kind of minute produces the most orders in the Lumen Glow data?

CTA 11.57, price 7.69, demo 7.72, urgency 5.83, build 4.51, Q&A 3.68, hook 1.98, filler 1.40, dead air 0.71. The ask leads by a wide margin and takes only 9.9 percent of the average night.

An urgency run converts at 15.12 percent in its first minute and 15.44 in its second. What happens in the third?

Runs 1 to 6 convert at 15.12, 15.44, 8.30, 8.33, 8.43, 9.09. Within stream, minutes 1 and 2 add 4.93 points; minute 3 onward costs 1.84. Two minutes, then move on.

Which of these urgency lines is compliant on a TikTok Shop LIVE?

TikTok Shop's misleading-content guide flags "While stocks last" and "Don't miss these big discounts" and "We are having our largest sale!"; the UK policy prohibits claiming the seller will soon stop selling when that is not the case; the Misleading Discount guide allows a time-limited claim exactly when it is genuine. A visible, real countdown is the compliant version.
